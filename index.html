Test it against the real market

Everything in the other tabs uses the past. Here the robot faces days that haven't happened yet, so it can't have memorized them. There are four ways, from simplest to most real:

  1. Forward test: lock in an algorithm today and it's scored only on the days after that. No sign-up.
  2. Live watch: real prices stream in during market hours and the robot decides as they move. Needs a free Finnhub or Alpaca key.
  3. Alpaca practice account: the robot places pretend orders with a real broker at real prices.
  4. Automatic daily bot: runs every trading day on its own, even with your laptop off.

    4. Automatic daily bot

    Checking…

    A scheduled GitHub job runs at about 11am New York time every trading day. It fetches the latest prices, runs your locked-in algorithm and places orders on your Alpaca practice account. It decides on yesterday's closing prices and trades that morning, which is exactly how the backtests work. Stop-loss and take-profit are sent to Alpaca as real orders, so they trigger even between runs.

    Set it up (one time)
    1. Store your keys in GitHub's locked storage. Open the repository's secrets page, press New repository secret and add two: ALPACA_KEY_ID (your Key ID) and ALPACA_SECRET_KEY (your secret key). Nobody else can read these, not even on the public repository.
    2. Choose what the bot trades. Pick forward tests below and give each a budget (dollars, or a share of the account), then press Make bot settings. You can change budgets later without starting over.
    3. Paste the settings into GitHub. Press Copy, open live/bot.json on GitHub, replace everything with what you copied, and press Commit changes.
    4. Done. To try it straight away during market hours, open the bot's page on GitHub and press Run workflow.

    How the Trading Lab works

    The robot

    A strategy is a set of buy rules and sell rules. Every trading day, after the market closes, the robot checks its rules against that day's prices. If the buy rules are met and it holds nothing, it buys at the next morning's opening price. If the sell rules are met while it holds the stock, it sells the next morning. Waiting until the next open matters: it means the robot never acts on a price it couldn't have known yet.

    It trades one stock at a time, only buys (no betting on prices falling), and holds cash when it's out of the market. Cash earns nothing, to keep things simple.

    Safety settings

    • Stop-loss: sell if the price falls a set % below what you paid. It triggers during the day, the moment the price touches the level.
    • Take-profit: sell once the price is a set % above what you paid.
    • Trailing stop: sell if the price falls a set % from its highest point since you bought. It "follows" the price up.
    • Time limit: sell after a set number of trading days, no matter what.
    • Money per trade: what share of your money goes into each purchase.

    Costs

    Every buy and every sell pays a fee (commission) and slippage, meaning your order fills a little worse than the quoted price. Leaving these at zero makes strategies that trade a lot look far better than they would be.

    The scores

    • Yearly growth (CAGR): the steady yearly rate that would turn your starting money into the final amount.
    • Worst drop (maximum drawdown): the biggest fall from a high point to a low point along the way. It's how much pain you'd have had to sit through.
    • Sharpe ratio: growth divided by how bumpy the ride was. Above 1 is very good for a single stock, and the S&P 500 alone is usually around 0.5.
    • Return ÷ drop: yearly growth divided by the worst drop.
    • Buy & hold: what you'd get by simply buying on day one and never selling. This is the number to beat.

    Why the "hidden exam" matters

    If you try thousands of settings, some will look amazing on the past purely by luck. That's like acing a test because you saw the answer key. The fix is to split the years. The tuner may only look at the older part. The winner is then tested on the recent part it never saw. If it does about as well there, it probably found something real. If it falls apart, it memorized noise.

    Signs of a trustworthy result: the exam score is close to the training score; many of the top 10 settings also pass the exam; and on the heatmap the best spot sits in a broad bright area, not a single lucky square.

    The researched bot: Crash shield

    This bot came from a research run on the lab's own data, using the same engine as every other strategy here, including fees and slippage. The aim was a bot that earns about as much as simply holding, with smaller crashes, and keeps doing that on years it never saw.

    How it was made. The years were split into three parts:

    • 2000–2014: designing and tuning.
    • 2015–2020: checking.
    • 2021–2026: a final exam, kept locked until the end and opened once.

    To avoid fitting one lucky stock, every design was tested on 10 funds: SPY, QQQ, DIA, IWM, VTI, XLE, XLF, XLK, TLT and GLD. A design only counted if it beat holding in both the design and check years. From the survivors, the settings were taken from a broad stable area rather than the single best score.

    What it does. It stays invested almost all the time. It sells only when both of these are true:

    • the price is more than 6% below its 200-day average;
    • the price fell more than 4% over the last 15 trading days.

    It buys back as soon as the price is above its 200-day average again, or the last 15 days are positive. Every number is a dial, so you can tune it yourself in Auto-tune.

    The honest verdict. Over 26 years it earned about the same as holding and cut the worst crash by about a third. On the S&P 500 fund, $10,000 became about $85,600 against $92,500 for holding, with a worst drop of 34% instead of 55%. It did not pass the final exam: in 2021–2026 it trailed holding. Think of it as insurance.

    • Where it paid off: slow crashes, like 2002 (−13% vs −22%) and 2008 (−17% vs −37%).
    • Where it cost you: sudden drops that bounced straight back, like 2011 (−22% vs +2%) and 2025 (+4% vs +18%).

    What else was tried.

    • Classic trend following and dip-buying looked excellent on 2000–2014: about 9–10% a year with half the crash. Both collapsed on the check years, at about 3.5% a year against 11.1%. That is the hidden exam doing its job.
    • Stop-losses and trailing stops made every design worse. They cut good positions short and the bot bought straight back in.
    • Nothing tested here reliably beat simply holding an index fund on return in every era. The most a rule offered was similar long-run growth with smaller crashes.

    The research scripts are in the research folder of the repository, if you want to check or extend the work.

    Honest limits

    • Survivorship bias: the stock list is made of companies that are big and successful today. A strategy tested on Nvidia or Apple benefits from knowing they survived. Index funds like SPY avoid most of this.
    • Daily prices only: one price per day (open, high, low, close). Nothing happens between days except stop-loss, take-profit and trailing-stop checks against the day's high and low.
    • Prices are adjusted for stock splits and dividends, so dividends are counted as if reinvested, for both the robot and buy & hold.
    • Past results don't guarantee future results. This is a learning tool, not financial advice.

    Where the prices come from

    Daily prices for 42 US stocks and funds are downloaded from Yahoo Finance every weeknight after the market closes, by a GitHub Action in this project's repository.
